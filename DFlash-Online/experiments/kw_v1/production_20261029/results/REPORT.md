## Answer

**KW did not demonstrate a useful overall improvement over K in this run, and added 41.1% measured training time.** The prespecified outcome is **mixed/inconclusive**: the new-panel primary is slightly positive, but the pooled final result is slightly negative. This is not evidence that the methods are equivalent, or that every learned-weight scheme must fail.

On the primary new 64-request panel, KW saves **six verification calls**: K uses 2,786 and KW 2,780, a **0.2154% reduction**. Summed mean request time falls from 48.0135 to 47.9030 seconds, a **0.2302% reduction**. Requests improve/regress/tie on 25/20/19, with 40 calls saved and 34 added. The paired-request bootstrap 95% interval is **−0.6897% to +1.0527%**, so this small observed gain does not establish a reliable advantage.

Across all four final panels, KW uses **eight more calls** than K: 11,442 versus 11,434, **0.0700% worse**. Request time is **0.0766% worse**. There are 93 improved, 98 regressed and 65 tied requests, with 160 calls saved and 168 added. The added work therefore did not buy an overall serving benefit in this screen.

K itself remains better than ordinary OSD: on the pooled final panels O/K/KW use **11,844 / 11,434 / 11,442 calls**. K reduces O's calls by **3.4617%** and request time by **3.3425%**. KW's gain over O does not establish value for the component added to K.

## Did the allocator actually change the updates?

Yes. After the two required neutral windows, 510/512 windows used nonzero allocation. Across all windows, the response weights remained close to equal weighting: **49.372%–50.628%**. The 504 delayed credits, state continuity and pass-boundary clearing all reconcile. This rules out a wholly inactive controller as the explanation, but does not establish why this small learned reallocation failed to improve overall serving. The observation does not authorize increasing its strength or retuning.
